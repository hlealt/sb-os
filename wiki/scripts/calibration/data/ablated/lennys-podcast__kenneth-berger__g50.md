# Why not asking for what you want is holding you back | Kenneth Berger

## Substance

Kenneth Berger (first PM at Slack, now an executive coach for startup leaders) argues that the core skill behind integrity, lower burnout, and effective influence is  — a three-step iterative loop: articulate what you want clearly and honestly, ask for it intentionally and humbly, then accept the response (often a "no") with emotional regulation and iterate.[^1] He defines integrity in this frame as the alignment between what you truly want, your honest expression of it, and your acceptance of the world's response — "living without fooling yourself about your real goals."[^1] The two failure modes are the people-pleaser (who suppresses desires and hopes others read their mind) and the entitled controller (who orders rather than asks); the skill is the humble middle path.[^1]

Three supporting models sharpen the loop.[^1]

Berger's actionable principles: articulate desires even imperfectly (tune into subtle emotions and complaints, balance realistic against inspiring, hold outcomes loosely); ask intentionally and humbly to preserve relationships; accept the "no" as data, not permanent rejection; and lean on relationships and vulnerability rather than data alone — PMs over-rely on hard data when their honest, trusted opinion already carries influence.[^1] For feedback he uses a nonviolent-communication / Radical Candor formula: state the observed behaviour (fact), name an actual feeling word, then the reason — avoiding judgmental stories.[^1] He recommends explicit "relationship design" conversations for a startup's first – hires to align expectations and working styles upfront.[^1]

His contrarian claims: discipline is unsustainable motivation ("discipline will get you in the gym for a week, but not for a year") — durable drive comes from wanting the thing, not self-punishment; and fear is a legacy survival response that is non-functional in modern office work ("fear is for when there's a tiger chasing you and there's no tiger chasing me"), so vision and joy are better fuel than fear.[^1] The lessons are grounded in his own war story: he was fired three times as Slack's first PM in 2014 — first for a mismatch of expectations and failure to articulate, then for relapsing into people-pleasing, then for being unable to hear and process the "no's" — the autobiographical proof that the asking loop is load-bearing.[^1]
